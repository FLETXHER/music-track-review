# Web Research Protocol

Web research is mandatory for each track. The goal is not to collect the most links; it is to obtain the right evidence for the exact recording.

## 1. Resolve identity and version

Confirm as needed:
- exact artist credit and track title;
- album/single/release context;
- release date and, when relevant, recording date;
- remix, live, alternate take, demo, edit, remaster, or version number;
- featured artists and producer/remixer identity.

Title similarity is not enough. A one-character difference can refer to a different recording.

## 2. Choose sources by evidence type

There is no single global source ranking. Use the source that is strongest for the fact being checked.

### Release identity, dates, credits, versions

Prefer:
1. artist, label, distributor, or official release pages;
2. MusicBrainz;
3. Discogs;
4. specialist storefront/database sources when appropriate;
5. reputable editorial sources.

### Fine-grained genre and style

High-value signals include:
- Rate Your Music (primary/secondary genres and descriptors) when the current tool can access it;
- AllMusic;
- MusicBrainz genres/tags;
- Discogs genre/style;
- Beatport for electronic and club-oriented releases;
- artist/label descriptions;
- reputable genre-focused publications.

Rate Your Music is useful for granular style vocabulary, but its user rating is not a Musicality score and must not influence the numeric ratings directly.

### Electronic / club music

Beatport and artist/label release pages are especially useful for:
- subgenre naming;
- BPM;
- mix/version names;
- club-market positioning.

Do not assume a storefront genre label is the only valid description of the track.

### Production, arrangement, performance, and sonic details

Use:
- official credits and liner-note information;
- interviews with artist/producer/engineer;
- MusicBrainz/Discogs credits;
- established editorial publications such as Pitchfork, Resident Advisor, The Guardian, The Fader, Consequence, and comparable outlets.

These sources are valuable when they describe concrete features such as instrumentation, samples, vocal processing, dynamics, arrangement, or performance.

## 3. Cross-check rather than vote

Do not count labels and choose the most frequent one.

Instead:
1. establish the recording identity;
2. gather one or more credible style signals;
3. gather enough musical evidence to support scoring;
4. resolve conflicts by asking which source is authoritative for that *kind of fact* and which description better matches the track.

For straightforward tracks, two strong evidence types may be sufficient. For ambiguous, hybrid, obscure, or disputed tracks, increase source diversity.

## 4. Do not let reviews set the score

Critical acclaim, aggregate ratings, chart success, awards, and cultural status can provide context but do not determine Mainstream Appeal or Musicality.

Translate concrete evidence into the scoring rubric:
- a review noting a sparse linear form may affect structural accessibility;
- confirmed layered brass/strings and dynamic development may support arrangement quality;
- praise alone is not a reason for 5/5.

## 5. Insufficient evidence

When reliable evidence is weak:
- use a broader Style label;
- state uncertainty only when it materially affects the answer;
- never fabricate credits, samples, dates, or style provenance.

If the host cannot access the web, stop the normal workflow and tell the user that web research is unavailable.
